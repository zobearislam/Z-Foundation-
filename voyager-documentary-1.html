Voyager

Two spacecraft, one alignment of planets that comes once in about 176 years, and a message for whoever finds them.

1977

In the mid-1960s a young JPL trajectory analyst named Gary Flandro noticed that Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune would line up in the late 1970s. A single spacecraft could swing from one planet to the next, each gravity assist bending its path and adding speed. A mission that would take 30 years by direct flight could take 12.

Voyager 2 launched on August 20, 1977. Voyager 1 followed on September 5, on a faster, shorter path.

Visual: a launch pad at dawn. Two Titan-Centaur rockets, sixteen days apart.

Why 1977

The alignment is a geometry problem. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune orbit at very different speeds, and only rarely sit along a curve a spacecraft can follow. The window that opened in the late 1970s would not return for about 176 years. Flandro spotted it in 1964, and his work became the basis of the Grand Tour idea.

From Mariner to Voyager

The full four-planet tour was too expensive in the early 1970s. NASA approved two spacecraft to visit Jupiter and Saturn, with the option of sending Voyager 2 onward if things went well. They were renamed Voyager shortly before launch.

Why number two flew first

Voyager 1 followed a faster, shorter path and overtook its twin. The numbers record the order of arrival at Jupiter, not the order of launch.

The hardware

  • About 800 kilograms each
  • A 3.7-metre dish and a 13-metre boom that holds the magnetometers away from the electronics
  • Three plutonium power generators instead of solar panels, because sunlight is too weak past Jupiter
  • Launched on Titan IIIE rockets with Centaur upper stages from Cape Canaveral

The first scare

In April 1978 Voyager 2 lost its primary radio receiver, and the backup had a faulty component that only accepted a very narrow frequency. Engineers have had to adjust their transmissions ever since to compensate for the Doppler shift. It still works.

March and July 1979

Voyager 1 reached Jupiter first and photographed a moon nobody understood. Io was speckled with color, and a navigation engineer, Linda Morabito, spotted a plume rising off its edge. It was the first active volcano found beyond Earth.

Voyager 2 followed months later and found lightning, a faint ring, and cracks in the ice of Europa that hinted at an ocean below.

Visual: the Io plume, then the Great Red Spot turning slowly in time-lapse.

The numbers

  • Voyager 1 passed about 349,000 km from the cloud tops on March 5, 1979
  • Voyager 2 passed about 570,000 km out on July 9, 1979
  • Each spacecraft flew through the harshest radiation belts of any planet and survived

Io, a moon turned inside out

Days before the encounter, three scientists published a prediction that tidal flexing by Jupiter and its other moons could keep Io's interior molten. Voyager 1 then found volcanoes erupting at once, the most volcanically active place known in the solar system. Sulfur and its compounds explain the orange, yellow and white surface.

Europa and the icy moons

Europa had almost no craters, so its surface must be young and being resurfaced. That, plus the cracks, first suggested a liquid ocean beneath the ice. Ganymede showed grooved terrain and Callisto was heavily cratered and ancient.

The planet itself

Voyager discovered a thin ring, found two small new moons, and watched lightning flash on the night side. The Great Red Spot proved to be a giant storm, wider than Earth, rotating counterclockwise. Time-lapse sequences let scientists see the turbulence around it for the first time.

Why it mattered

Jupiter's gravity gave each spacecraft a large boost in speed and bent its path toward Saturn. Without this assist, the tour would have taken far longer.

1980 and 1981

Saturn's rings turned out to be thousands of ringlets, braided and stirred by tiny moons. Voyager 1 also flew close to Titan, whose thick orange haze hid the surface completely. That flyby bent the probe out of the plane of the planets for good.

Voyager 2 used Saturn's gravity to continue on toward Uranus.

Visual: the rings backlit, dark against a bright limb.

The numbers

  • Voyager 1 passed about 124,000 km from Saturn's cloud tops on November 12, 1980
  • Voyager 2 followed on August 25, 1981

The rings, up close

From Earth the rings had looked like a few broad bands. Voyager resolved them into hundreds of ringlets. It found dark radial spokes in the B ring, a braided and kinked F ring, and small shepherd moons such as Prometheus and Pandora that hold ring edges in place.

Titan

Voyager 1 flew within about 6,400 km of Titan, the only moon in the solar system with a dense atmosphere. It measured a thick nitrogen atmosphere with methane, with a surface pressure higher than Earth's. The cameras never saw the ground. To get this view, mission planners gave up a possible later flyby of Pluto.

A hexagon at the pole

Voyager's images revealed a six-sided jet stream circling Saturn's north pole. It was still there decades later.

Voyager 2's problem

After Saturn, the camera platform on Voyager 2 jammed. Engineers on the ground worked out how to free it, and the spacecraft carried on to Uranus and Neptune with its cameras working.

1986 and 1989

Only Voyager 2 went on. At Uranus, in January 1986, it found ten new moons and a tilted, off-center magnetic field. At Neptune, in August 1989, it found the Great Dark Spot, supersonic winds, and geysers on the moon Triton.

No other spacecraft has visited either planet since.

Visual: two pale blue-green discs, filling the frame one after the other.

Uranus, January 24, 1986

  • Closest approach about 81,500 km above the cloud tops
  • Ten new moons and two new rings, bringing the ring count to eleven
  • The planet rotates on its side, with its axis tilted about 98 degrees
  • The magnetic field is tilted about 59 degrees from the rotation axis and offset from the center

The atmosphere looked nearly blank in visible light. The most dramatic find was Miranda, a small moon of jumbled terrain, with a cliff some 20 kilometres high. The encounter took place four days before the Challenger accident.

Neptune, August 25, 1989

  • Passed just a few thousand kilometres above the north pole, the closest approach to any planet on the tour
  • Six new moons, and ring arcs that turned out to be complete but uneven rings
  • The Great Dark Spot, a storm about the size of Earth, and winds above 2,000 km/h, the fastest known in the solar system

Triton

Triton orbits Neptune backwards, which suggests it was captured from the Kuiper belt. Voyager measured a surface temperature near minus 235 degrees Celsius and saw dark plumes of nitrogen rising from the ice. Later observations found that the Great Dark Spot had disappeared, so Neptune's weather changes far faster than expected.

The end of the tour

Neptune's gravity swung Voyager 2 southward, out of the plane of the planets. It is still heading that way today.

February 14, 1990

Past Neptune the planetary mission was over. Carl Sagan asked for one more thing: turn Voyager 1's camera around and photograph the planets. It took sixty frames from about six billion kilometers away.

In one of them, Earth is less than a pixel, caught in a beam of scattered sunlight.

Visual: hold on the single pixel for ten seconds. Silence. Then narration.

How it happened

Carl Sagan, a member of the imaging team, proposed the photograph in the late 1980s. Some engineers worried that pointing the camera near the Sun could damage it, and that the spacecraft's limited power was better spent elsewhere. NASA's administrator supported the idea, and the pictures were taken about a quarter of a century after the first planetary flybys had begun.

The family portrait

  • Sixty frames in all, forming a mosaic of the solar system
  • Six planets appear: Venus, Earth, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune
  • Mercury was lost in the Sun's glare, and Mars was hidden in scattered light
  • Earth measures about a tenth of a pixel across

The sunbeam

The band of light crossing the image is scattered sunlight inside the camera, not a feature of space. It happens to place Earth inside a ray of light.

The last pictures

Soon afterwards the cameras were switched off to save power and computer memory for the long journey. They have never been used again. Sagan wrote about the image in a book published in 1994, and it remains one of the most reproduced pictures ever taken.

2012 and 2018

The Sun blows a bubble of charged particles through space, the heliosphere. Voyager 1 left it in August 2012, when its instruments saw the plasma density jump by a factor of about 40. Voyager 2 crossed in November 2018 on a different path, giving scientists a second look at the boundary.

They are the only human-made objects to travel through interstellar space.

Visual: a diagram of the bubble, with two thin lines passing through its wall.

Three boundaries

  • Termination shock: where the solar wind slows abruptly. Voyager 1 crossed it in December 2004, at about 94 AU. Voyager 2 crossed in August 2007, at about 84 AU.
  • Heliosheath: the turbulent region beyond it, where the wind is slow and hot.
  • Heliopause: the outer edge, where the Sun's influence gives way to interstellar space.

How they knew

Voyager 1's plasma instrument had stopped working in 1980, so it could not directly measure the surrounding gas. Instead, scientists used its plasma wave receiver to listen to the gas ring after a solar outburst. The pitch gave the density, and the density gave the answer: interstellar space.

Voyager 2 confirms

Voyager 2 crossed on November 5, 2018, at about 119 AU. Its plasma instrument was still working, so it could measure the change directly. The two crossings happened at different latitudes and at different points in the solar cycle, and were not at the same distance, which showed the bubble is not a simple sphere.

What is out there

Beyond the edge, cosmic rays from distant supernovae are far more intense, and the magnetic field runs in a different direction. The Voyagers are still sampling that environment, and no other spacecraft can.

Aboard both spacecraft

Each carries a gold-plated copper disc, designed by a team led by Carl Sagan: spoken greetings in 55 languages, 115 images of Earth, the sound of a kiss, a heartbeat, rain, and 90 minutes of music from many cultures.

Its cover shows how to play it, and where the Sun is, using pulsars as landmarks. The chance of anyone finding it is tiny. That was never the point. It was a statement about who we wanted to be.

Visual: the record, turning slowly. Layer in fragments of its own sounds.

Who made it

A small team led by Carl Sagan built the record in about six months. It included Frank Drake, Ann Druyan, Jon Lomberg, Linda Salzman Sagan and the producer Timothy Ferris.

What is on it

  • Greetings in 55 languages, and a message from Jimmy Carter and from the United Nations Secretary-General
  • 115 images of Earth, life, people and science
  • Sounds of nature and people at work: wind, thunder, birds, whales, a kiss, laughter, a mother and child, footsteps, a train, and a rocket
  • About 90 minutes of music, from Bach and Mozart to Chuck Berry, and folk and traditional music from many cultures, including Navajo, Peruvian and Indian recordings
  • An hour of recorded brain activity and a heartbeat, from Ann Druyan

The cover

The instructions are drawn on the case. They show the needle, the spinning speed, and how to decode the images. The map uses the pulse rates of 14 pulsars to show where the Sun is. A thin patch of uranium-238 on the cover acts as a clock, because its half-life is about 4.5 billion years.

How long it lasts

The gold-plated copper discs are expected to survive for a billion years or more, far longer than anything on Earth's surface.

1970s hardware, 2020s mission

The computers aboard the Voyagers hold less memory than a single small image on a phone. The spacecraft run on plutonium, talk with a transmitter about as strong as a small light bulb, and have been repaired by radio for decades.

Computing, 1970s style

Each spacecraft has three computers, with roughly 70 kilobytes of memory between them. A phone photo is thousands of times larger. Data was stored on a digital tape recorder holding a few dozen megabytes, and one picture took minutes to transmit.

Power

  • Three radioisotope generators turn the heat of decaying plutonium into electricity
  • They produced about 470 watts at launch and lose around 4 watts every year
  • The transmitter draws about as much as a small light bulb

Instruments

Each craft carries about a dozen instruments: cameras, infrared and ultraviolet spectrometers, magnetometers, and detectors for plasma, radio waves and cosmic rays. Some have already failed or been switched off, but the ones that remain are measuring the interstellar medium.

Speed and distance

Voyager 1 travels at about 17 km/s and Voyager 2 at about 15 km/s. Signals are received by the 70-metre dishes of NASA's Deep Space Network. Because of its southward path, Voyager 2 can only be commanded from the Canberra station, and during a long upgrade there in 2020 and 2021 it could not receive new commands for months.

Repairs from 24 billion kilometres

In late 2023 Voyager 1 began sending unreadable data because of a fault in its flight data computer. In 2024 engineers moved the affected code to another part of the memory and restored the data. They have also revived thrusters that had not been used for decades. Every fix is sent as a radio command that takes a day to arrive and a day to be confirmed.

2026

Voyager 1 is roughly 170 astronomical units from the Sun and closing in on a distance of one light-day, about 25 billion kilometers. A radio signal takes a full day to reach it. Voyager 2 is not far behind.

Both run on plutonium that loses a few watts every year. Engineers switch off instruments one at a time to keep the essentials alive, and most of the science payload is expected to fall silent in the coming years. After that, the spacecraft keep going without us.

In about 40,000 years, Voyager 1 will pass within light-years of another star.

Visual: the dark. One faint radio line drifting across it. Fade out.

Running out of power

With the generators weakening, engineers have been turning instruments off one by one since 2024 to keep the heaters, computers and transmitter running. The goal is to keep at least one instrument on each spacecraft returning data for as long as possible, and both are expected to fall silent at some point in the 2030s.

One light-day

NASA projects that Voyager 1 will reach one light-day from Earth in November 2026, about 25 billion kilometres. From then on a command takes more than 24 hours to arrive, and the reply takes another day.

After the last signal

  • Voyager 1 will pass within about 1.6 light-years of the star Gliese 445 in roughly 40,000 years
  • Voyager 2 will pass about 1.7 light-years from Ross 248 at around the same time
  • Both will keep orbiting the center of the galaxy long after the Sun has changed

What it changed

The Voyagers gave us close-up pictures of four giant planets and dozens of moons, showed that the outer solar system is active, not frozen, and opened the study of the heliosphere. They also carry our first attempt at a letter to the universe.
